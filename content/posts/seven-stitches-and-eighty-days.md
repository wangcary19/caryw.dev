Shit happens.

… and especially when it least needs to, as per Murphy’s Law. It’s the unspoken baseline of engineering; no matter the sub-discipline, every system and solution is designed with leeway and operating capacity for probabilistic failure, in part or in its entirety. A dam must have spillways, a bridge with room to sway in the wind, an internet server with failover protocol. Best practices are mandated and standardized, the continuous culmination of lessons learned from histories of errors. Yet, when it comes to the failures of individuals in the system of society - arguably the longest-running human-designed system - good engineering practices are sparse, improving upon mistakes is a foreign concept, and there is little to no standard protocol.

On a Thursday in August, after falling forward during a bike ride, I unwillingly dragged myself to the hospital to receive seven stitches on the chin. The next morning, I woke to an email informing me of layoffs. After a short, grayscale call at lunchtime, I was left at my desk battered and bruised and jobless, with only about 80 days of financial runway à-la-severance. What followed were weeks of slogging through job applications, recruiter calls, and interview preparation as the difference between weekends and weekdays blurred, and the accoutrements of daily life became self-imposed, mandatory breaks from the never-ending cycle of wake up, apply, and sleep. Yet, pushing me on ever so strongly, was the ominous, looming proposition of the inability of the current societal system to robustly absorb and resolve my personal failure. By the end of the year, if no opportunities followed through, I would be homeless and financially defunct.

I desperately applied to local state social services for unemployment support but was met by a woefully archaic portal and application process, which required at least a few weeks of profile review for eligibility, with the earliest availability for tangible financial support two months away. What’s more, parts of the certification required evidence delivered via snail mail, as if to add insult to injury. It felt as if even the help system was seeking to reject me as well.

Okay, some half-truths there. My mother, who did not mince words about the difficulties ahead, had already offered a warm and welcoming room at home (not to mention, a medical bill bailout, if insurance denied the claims post-layoffs). The assurance of familial security became the key stronghold in the mental uphill battle to follow, as rejection after rejection arrived. Knowing that my 80-day runway was not followed by immediate doomsday, the immense pressure of the job search eased and the craters left by each “_We appreciate you taking the effort to apply, but…_” made shallower pockets in resolve. It was the confidence in knowing that this personal failure was still in a recoverable position that allowed for continued advances into the overall effort.

By sheer luck and opportune timing, unemployment lasted a little under a month. But what of those who are less fortunate, both in the search for opportunities as well as the support underlying? What was obvious now was the ineptitude of the meager system in place to catch those who fall, flail, and fail - the dangling of unemployment benefits with a two-month buffer was somewhat troublesome already with a three-month runway, but what happens to the majority[1](#footnote-1) living closer to the margins? Consider the harrowing reality that when that unemployment does arrive, it may cover less than 40% of expected income.[2](#footnote-2) Displacement could have been a devastating loss of home and family, and the start of a Sisyphean cycle of debt and despair. Should those who fail, be it of their own accord or the happenstances of worldly affairs, not be offered a stable chance and comfortable platform from which to pick up from their learning or get back on their feet?

A hardline “bootstrapping” ethos dominates the mainstream, through which hard effort unadulterated by “freebies” is venerated. Media and business education is rife with zero-to-hundred, garage-to-mansion case studies, and publicly successful persons are incentivized to downplay their stories in pursuit of social norms of humility and “self-starterism.”[3](#footnote-3) While in theory this promotes a culture of hustle, in truth those who have successfully bootstrapped are too quick to downplay the importance of opportune timings and the shifting of the trade winds. I was one of them - I had grown complacent in the golden window of post-COVID employment. Had I graduated one year earlier, the job market could have fielded tumbleweeds; one year later, and the advent of AI would erase and redefine entry-level positions across industries. Good luck comes to those who have the _capability_ to wait and pounce. Just as climbers stay in portaledges to abate the storm before a summit bid, so should we strive to offer safe passage for the willing and able to stage their respective bids at success.

There exists no panacea or playbook for reforming the current systems, but we can always look to the state of continuous experimentation worldwide, and apply the most effectual reforms to existing systems. These include but are not limited to the decoupling of healthcare from employment, modernization of state unemployment services, and dynamic unemployment insurance and severance - three major, archaic points of difficulty that felt closest to home over this summer’s month of unemployment. In general, a paradigm shift with regards to the unemployment safety net is required in policy-making: new technologies arrive, markets shift, and careers will inevitably be lost to time as human advancement progresses. We need not be Luddites in response to shifts in employment, yet nor should we adopt a laissez-faire, borderline negligent, and mildly accusatory attitude towards the unemployed; instead, in recognizing that job loss is just as much a part of overall employment and a successful career, we must build the foundational, “failure-tolerant” systems that help people continue moving both upwards and forwards during times of transition. ♦
