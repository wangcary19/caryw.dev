I had my first ever concussion.

I had planned to go my entire life without having one, alas Murphy and his law made a devious intervention. It happened in an instant - first, the torrential rain, a gift of relief from a summer afternoon storm cell. A flooding underground mezzanine, with subway-goers removing shoes to pass barefoot through the growing water. Glasses off, shirt off, and the desperate sprint to a nearby friend’s residence. Droplets so thick and heavy that the surface of the earth was wiped clean of any detail. A step on slippery granite pavement, chipped elbows, and a ferocious whiplash knock to the back of the head. And the cherry on top, a group of onlooking French tourists who witnessed the tumble applauding with a picture-perfect “ooh! là-là!”

Adrenaline brought me piecemeal chez friend, where fortunately his longtime partner in medicine was able to assuage the growing panic of a possibly life-threatening situation in a level-headed survey (no thunderclap headache, no loss of consciousness, no loss of motor control). I sat down, sopping wet and somewhat dazed, gently caressing the back of my skull - tender, but relatively unfazed. Someone poured a glass of iced Diet Coke, and we got about to a normal conversation, as normal as a conversation can be knowing one party has just suffered a traumatic brain injury (TBI). They were leaving for a trip soon, and well-wishes for the journey were reciprocated with well-wishes for my head. I trained home in a damp silence, jittering occasionally in the seat as the last waves of panic washed out.

_No brain bleed, I’ll survive._  
  
But surviving is an experience entirely separate from living, and in the days that followed, living became surviving, and surviving became a full-time job. What can only be described as at first a thin, summer haze descending on the conscience then became an overcast drizzle, and subsequently a thick fog. A persistent sense of imbalance and bouts of vertigo accompanied the slowdown of the conscious, compounding and turning routine morning coffee rounds into whiskeyed stupors - sorties made stumbling and mumbling, in broad daylight with the smell of Guatemalan dark roast in place of alcohol. Friends and colleagues conversed with a goldfish that had run into the walls of its own bowl; I became a comedian’s dream audience, what with ideas and jokes delivered recyclable within the half hour to no better hint of recollection. Once-cohesive thoughts became unfathomably remixed storylines broken by awkward pauses. It was as if an infestation of hares, gophers, moles, voles, field mice… all manner of small burrowing mammals had dug through the field of memories, leaving a patchy plot dotted with pits and potholes. I even went on a date, the last half spent relearning the conversations of the first (she was okay with the experience initially, then turned down a second date).

More concerning than the presenting symptoms, however, was the overwhelming sense that I was somehow _lesser_ than before the whiplash. Having known and expected the acuity of faculties, the sudden combination of vertigo and short-term memory loss cast a continuous discombobulation. Unmoving walls and steady floors - former waystones that I had taken for granted. A walk through a door frame or a flash of a light too bright, and it was as if the _Men in Black_ had used their memory-wiping devices. I knew I should’ve been able to, but in the end I no longer could. My own conscious experience, assembled as an emergent property of the senses, was frustratingly damaged. Did I really ever know that the world was not moving with every step? Was it always possible to remember the contents of more than two messages at once? Was I always like this?

It’s a harrowing realization; first, the anxiety of the possibility of never again returning to a previous baseline norm, and second, the reality of losing oneself so easily amidst the diminishment of worldly faculties. A concussion is a ticket to living purgatory, where the new normalcy is a perpetual in-between of worrying about the future, wrangling with the present, and wishing for the past. It breaks the fourth wall of the lived experience, for the “consciousness” that man so prides himself upon as the great distinction between animal and human is made out to be nothing more than a construct of his senses, precariously positioned upon a limited vantage of health and wellbeing. What we know about ourselves is built on a foundation of sand - a single life-shifting earthquake away from liquefaction.

Who are we to say who we are, when the answer to this question is liable to change day-to-day, person-to-person? We can’t. The best foot forward, then, is to give friends, family, colleagues… a wide berth. Their circumstance now is unknown to us, and their situation tomorrow even less so. In the weeks since injury, the people around me have given me ample room for error, for which I am eternally grateful. Time spent together cut short, plans upended midway, and an endless circle of “sorry, could you repeat that?”, yet the quality of compassion ever-present nevertheless, justifies the need and ethics of treating fellow man with understanding, even when their condition lays unbeknownst to many. Need to step away for a headache? No questions asked. Plans made months ago suddenly need altering? That’s okay, we can accommodate. Can’t talk right now, world is spinning? Take it easy. A mutual understanding of fragility seeds a general compassion, and this magnanimity thus ensures our shared _wellbeings_. ■
